Executive summary

Most simulator lounges sell the same three things: bay time, beer, and a burger. That business works in winter and limps through summer, and every one of them competes on the same Instagram ads. Fairway Forward runs two engines under one roof. The first is a premium lounge for golfers who already play: members get priority booking windows, leagues, coaching on real launch-monitor data, and a kitchen and bar worth staying for. The second is an academy, run by a separate nonprofit, that buys the bays during the hours no adult golfer wants them, weekday mornings and afternoons and the dead summer months, and fills them with students from school divisions that serve mostly Black and brown families.

The two engines are not in tension. They are complementary on the calendar and complementary on the P&L. Members and the public pay for nights and weekends. Schools, grant funders and sponsors pay for weekdays. The Foundation covers roughly a third of the Director of Golf's salary and buys bay time at a contracted rate, so the mission is a customer, not a charity line.

The core opinion in this plan: open with four bays, a limited kitchen and a beer-and-wine license, not two bays. The fixed costs that make this concept work (a general manager, a PGA professional, a kitchen that satisfies Virginia ABC, a lease with a classroom) are the same at two bays and four. Two bays never clear debt service in the model. Four bays reach 1.4x coverage in Year 3 and 2.1x by Year 5.

What is different about it

  • Preferred tee times as a product. Booking windows are tiered (7, 10, 14 days out) so a member who pays more always gets the Tuesday 6pm slot. That is the club-membership psychology, applied indoors.
  • A curriculum, not a clinic. Launch-monitor data is a math and physics lab. Turfgrass and weather are earth science. The golf economy is a careers unit. The academy maps modules to Virginia Standards of Learning and Career and Technical Education pathways so a principal can justify the bus.
  • Careers beyond the Tour. Superintendent, club professional, general manager, club fitter, turf equipment technician, golf course architect, rules official, sports marketer, hospitality manager. Most pay well, many are short-staffed, and almost none have a recruiting pipeline into Hampton or Newport News.
  • Services golfers actually want. Sports massage and sports pedicure suites, rented to newly licensed practitioners, sit next to the bays. No other lounge in the market does this.
  • A path to a campus. Phase 3 is a converted big box with real bunkers, a putting green, an education wing and event space. The financing tools for that, New Markets Tax Credits, Opportunity Zone equity, SBA 504, are ones you already understand as a developer.

Two engines, one roof

OpCo · Fairway Forward LLC (for-profit)

  • Owns the lease, the simulators, the kitchen, the ABC license, the brand.
  • Sells hourly bay time, memberships, leagues, lessons, events, F&B, retail, wellness suite rent.
  • Employs the GM, the Director of Golf & Education, kitchen and floor staff.
  • Sells bay time and half the Director's time to the Foundation at contracted rates.

Foundation · Fairway Forward Academy (501(c)(3))

  • Year 1 under a fiscal sponsor (a community foundation) while the IRS determination is pending; stands alone from Year 2.
  • Contracts with school divisions, applies for grants, signs corporate sponsors, runs the annual fundraising scramble.
  • Pays OpCo for bay-hours, the Director's academy time, and the classroom.
  • Owns the curriculum, the scholarship fund and the career-pathway relationships.

Keep the wall between them clean. The Foundation's board needs independent members (a school administrator, a PGA professional from a local club, a corporate sponsor, a Hampton University faculty member), and the contract between the two entities has to be at fair market value. Funders will ask, and a clean structure is what lets a bank give the OpCo credit for the education revenue while a community foundation gives the Foundation a grant.

The rest of the golfer product

Leagues

Three eight-week seasons (fall, winter, spring), four-player teams, $250 a player. Six teams per bay-night. League nights are the highest F&B hours of the week.

Coaching on data

A PGA or LPGA professional as Director of Golf. Lessons at $100 an hour on a 50/50 split. Every member gets a launch-monitor profile: club speed, ball speed, smash factor, launch angle, spin, dispersion. Quarterly progress reports keep the renewal.

Club fitting and retail

Fitting days with a shaft and head matrix; a small retail wall (gloves, balls, apparel, a fitting-partner brand). Retail is small money but high trust.

Corporate and events

Full buyouts at $350 to $500 an hour with F&B minimums. The classroom doubles as a meeting room. Target Newport News Shipbuilding suppliers, Langley contractors, banks, healthcare systems and law firms.

Technology choice

The model budgets $52,000 per bay for a Foresight GCQuad or GCHawk class system with enclosure, impact screen, projector, turf, PC and software. Trackman iO bays run $70,000 and up. Trackman carries more prestige with low-handicap players; Foresight measures club data as well and costs less. Pick one platform and standardize so members' data follows them bay to bay. Get quotes for both before the SBA application.

The Academy: golf as a classroom and a career

An Achievable Dream in Newport News started in 1992 as a tennis program and grew into full academies because it tied a sport to academics and character, and because it built a durable relationship with the school division. This academy does the same with golf, with one added layer: the golf industry is a large employer with a workforce shortage and almost no pipeline into communities like Hampton and Newport News. Superintendents, assistant pros, equipment technicians and club managers are hired every season. Very few of the people hired look like the students in these schools.

Program formats

Field-trip blocks

Two-hour blocks for 16 to 24 students, four bays plus the classroom. One curriculum module per visit. Priced to the division at about $600 a block, mostly grant-reimbursed.

After-school cohorts

Eight-week cohorts, twice a week, 12 to 16 students. Golf skills plus one academic module plus one careers session. Transportation is the make-or-break cost; budget it.

Summer academies

One-week day camps, June to August, sliding-scale fees with Foundation scholarships. This is what fills the bays in the months the adult market dies.

High school teams and a Peninsula junior league

Winter practice contracts for school golf teams that have no indoor option. A junior league in the spring feeds the membership base a decade out.

Work-based learning

Paid roles at the facility for 16 to 18 year olds that count for CTE credit: bay tech, front desk, kitchen, junior coach. The facility becomes its own first employer.

Career days and site visits

A superintendent day at a local course, a Virginia Tech turfgrass visit, a fitting-van day, a Hampton University golf practice, an industry panel. Two a semester.

Partners to line up

  • School divisions: Hampton City Schools, Newport News Public Schools first; then Portsmouth, Norfolk, Suffolk. Enter through the CTE director and the Title IV-A coordinator, not the athletic department.
  • An Achievable Dream: a partner and a model, not a competitor. Offer their students a golf elective before you approach anyone else.
  • First Tee Hampton Roads: partner for the youngest ages and share curriculum; position the academy as the careers and STEM layer on top of their life-skills work.
  • Hampton University: the golf team, the business school, and the alumni network for donors and mentors.
  • PGA REACH and PGA WORKS, the USGA, the Virginia State Golf Association Foundation: grants, scholarships and fellowships aimed at exactly this population.
  • GCSAA First Green and Virginia Tech turfgrass: the earth science curriculum and the superintendent pipeline.
  • Local clubs: a caddie and junior-employment agreement with two or three private clubs unlocks the Evans Scholars route.

Site criteria

  • Second-generation retail or flex with 11-foot clear height. Former fitness, furniture, or restaurant space converts cheapest. Restaurant space brings a hood and grease trap, worth $40,000 or more.
  • Within 15 minutes of both Hampton and Newport News schools; on a bus-accessible corridor (Mercury Boulevard and Coliseum Central in Hampton, Jefferson Avenue and Oyster Point in Newport News are the obvious corridors to vet).
  • A 7- to 10-year lease with a TI allowance. The model assumes $17 base rent, $4.50 NNN and $20 per square foot of TI. Your landlord relationships are worth real money here.
  • Check whether the parcel sits in an Opportunity Zone or a qualified low-income census tract now; it matters for Phase 3 financing and it may matter for local incentives in Phase 1.

Food, beverage and the ABC license

Serious golfers stay for a drink; leagues run on it. F&B is 27% of Year 3 revenue in the model, in line with mature lounges. But in Virginia the kitchen is not a nice-to-have: an on-premises license of any kind requires operating as a restaurant that serves meals, and a mixed beverage (liquor) license requires meeting a food-to-alcohol sales ratio. The historical requirement is that at least 45% of gross sales come from food and non-alcoholic beverages. Confirm the current ratio and fees with Virginia ABC before signing the lease, because it dictates kitchen size and menu.

Phase 1: beer and wine on-premises

Apply as soon as the lease is signed; approval runs 60 to 90 days with local notice requirements. Draft beer, canned cocktails, a short wine list. Lower food ratio burden, faster approval, lower liquor-liability premium.

Phase 2: mixed beverage restaurant

Convert once the kitchen has a track record and the food ratio is provable. Bourbon and golf go together; expect F&B per bay-hour to rise from about $20 to $30.

Menu

Smash burgers, wings, flatbreads, loaded fries, a salad, a kids' menu for academy families. Twelve items. Everything cooks on a flat-top or in a fryer so a two-person kitchen can run it.

Guardrails

No alcohol service during school blocks; the bar closes physically (roll-down or gate) from 9am to 3pm on program days. Write that into the school contracts before they ask.

Wellness services golfers will pay for

Sports massage, sports pedicures, and eventually stretch and mobility sessions belong next to a golf bay; nobody in the market offers them. The right model is suite rent plus a revenue share to a licensed practitioner, not employees, and not students.

Licensing reality. In Virginia, massage therapists are licensed by the Board of Nursing and nail technicians by the Board for Barbers and Cosmetology. Students may only perform services in a licensed school clinic under supervision. So "up and coming" practitioners means newly licensed graduates on their first chair, not students. If you have a relationship with a barber, beauty or massage school, the cleaner long-term move is for that school to license a satellite clinic inside the Phase 3 campus, which lets students serve golfers legally with the school's oversight.

  • Terms: $900 a month suite rent plus 15% of service revenue, month to month for the first six months, then a one-year agreement. One suite in Year 2, two by Year 4 in the model, about $14,400 per suite per year to the OpCo.
  • Booking: integrated into the member app so a 60-minute bay session and a 30-minute sports massage book as one package.
  • Insurance: practitioners carry their own professional liability; OpCo's general liability names them as additional insureds.

What moves the number

  • Utilization: each five points of utilization is worth about $35,000 of Year 3 EBITDA at $45 an hour (Sensitivity sheet).
  • F&B attach: every $5 of F&B per used bay-hour is worth about $26,000 of gross profit a year at four bays.
  • Rent and TI: $3 per square foot of rent is $14,700 a year, every year. A $10 per square foot bump in TI allowance is $49,000 off the loan.
  • Payroll: hourly labor is the largest line after rent. The model staffs to demand (75% of mature staffing in Year 1).

Growth roadmap

Phase 1
Months 0 to 24

Four-bay flagship, one classroom, one wellness suite

Prove three things: members renew above 75%, at least two school divisions sign a second-year contract, and the Foundation raises more than it spends. Convert to a mixed beverage license in Year 2. Add the second wellness suite. Hit 40% utilization.

Funding stack

Phase 1 (about $1.13M)

  • SBA 7(a) term loan, about $811,000. Ten-year blended term on equipment and leasehold improvements. Lenders will want a signed lease, quotes for the simulators, the founding-member pre-sale evidence and your personal guarantee. TowneBank, Old Point and the local SBA preferred lenders know the market.
  • Owner equity, about $227,000 (20%). Some lenders accept 10% for an experienced operator; the model uses 20% to be safe.
  • Founding memberships, $96,000. Deferred revenue on the books, cash in the bank.
  • Local incentives to pursue: Hampton and Newport News economic development grants for jobs and facade or interior improvements; Virginia Enterprise Zone job creation and real property investment grants if the site qualifies; Virginia Small Business Financing Authority loan guaranty as an alternative to SBA.
  • Landlord TI. Every dollar of TI is a dollar off the loan. Trade term length for TI.

Foundation (about $115,000 in Year 1)

  • A fiscal sponsor (the Hampton Roads Community Foundation or the Peninsula Community Foundation of Virginia) so grants can flow before the 501(c)(3) determination arrives.
  • School division contracts funded by Title IV-A (well-rounded education and enrichment), 21st Century Community Learning Centers, and CTE work-based learning budgets.
  • PGA REACH, PGA WORKS, USGA grants, the Virginia State Golf Association Foundation.
  • Corporate sponsors with a Community Reinvestment Act motive (banks) or a workforce motive (Newport News Shipbuilding and its suppliers, Sentara, Dominion Energy, Langley contractors).

Phase 3 (about $4M to $7M)

  • SBA 504 for owner-occupied real estate at a fixed rate, if you buy the box.
  • New Markets Tax Credits if the site is in a qualified low-income census tract; the education and workforce story is exactly what community development entities look for.
  • Opportunity Zone equity from investors with capital gains to defer; the program was renewed under 2025 federal tax legislation with new tract designations rolling out, so check the target parcel's status at the time.
  • HUD Section 108 through the city, city economic development authority participation, Virginia Enterprise Zone grants, and CRA-motivated bank debt.
  • Philanthropic capital for the education wing specifically: naming rights, a capital campaign run by the Foundation.

Council review

Five advisers, one chairman, as you asked for in your brief. They argued the plan as written.

ContrarianWhat will fail

The model assumes 60 members at the end of Year 1 and 40 founders before opening. Founders are sold on a rendering and your name; if you get 20, the reserve is gone by month 14. The academy is a cost center with a nonprofit wrapper until a division actually signs, and divisions sign slowly, so year one will feel like a bar with a classroom nobody uses. And the wellness suites are a distraction at four bays; do them in Phase 3.

First principlesWhat is true underneath

A bay is a fixed asset that earns only when a body is in it. The business is a utilization problem with a 14-hour window, and adult demand only exists for about six of those hours. Any concept that fills the other eight at any positive rate wins on the same rent. Schools are the only customer in the market who want the empty hours. So the academy is not a mission bolted onto a lounge; it is the correct answer to the utilization problem, and the mission is a bonus.

ExpansionistWhere the upside is

Nobody owns "the golf career pipeline for Black and brown kids in Virginia." Get two divisions and Hampton University on board and the Foundation becomes fundable at a scale that dwarfs the lounge: PGA REACH, USGA, the shipyard, the banks. The campus in Phase 3 is not a bigger lounge; it is a regional workforce asset, and that is what NMTC and city money fund. The curriculum itself is licensable to other lounges nationally.

OutsiderKnows nothing about golf

Why would a 14-year-old care about golf? The plan sells careers and math to principals but says little about what makes the kid come back on a Tuesday. Make the first visit a competition with a scoreboard and a prize, let them hit a driver as hard as they can, and give them the data on their phone. Also: parents. Where is the parent product? A parent watching from the lounge with a drink is a member you have not sold yet.

Get one school division signed before opening even if the first contract is small; a signed $24,000 contract is worth more to a lender and a funder than a $96,000 forecast. The plan's structural insight, that schools are the only buyer for the dead hours, is correct and is the reason this can outlast the other lounges in the market.

First 90 days

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: entities and people. Form the LLC. Engage counsel for the Foundation and the OpCo-Foundation services agreement. Approach a community foundation about fiscal sponsorship. Start the Director of Golf & Education search through the Middle Atlantic PGA Section.
  2. Weeks 1 to 4: technology and site. Quotes from Foresight and Trackman for four bays. Tour candidate spaces in Hampton and Newport News with your broker; screen for 11-foot clear height, hood or grease trap, and TI appetite. Pull census tract and Opportunity Zone status on every candidate.
  3. Weeks 2 to 6: schools. Meetings with the CTE director and Title IV-A coordinator at Hampton City Schools and Newport News Public Schools. Bring the curriculum map and a one-page block pricing sheet. Ask for a pilot: two field-trip blocks a month in the spring semester after opening. Meet An Achievable Dream's leadership and First Tee Hampton Roads.
  4. Weeks 3 to 8: money. Lender conversations with two SBA preferred lenders using this plan and the model. Confirm ABC requirements and fees with Virginia ABC. Ask the city's economic development office what it can do for a site on its corridor.
  5. Weeks 4 to 12: founding members. Landing page, rendering, a founders event at a local club. Goal: 30 signed by lease execution, 40 by opening. Track it weekly; it is the demand signal for everything else.
  6. Week 10 to 12: decide. Lease terms, lender term sheet, founders count and one signed or verbally committed school pilot in hand. If three of four are there, sign. If not, the plan says why, and you have lost 90 days and a few thousand dollars instead of a million.

Appendix

Things to verify before financing

  • Virginia ABC: current food-to-alcohol ratio for mixed beverage restaurant licenses, current license fees, local notice and hearing timelines.
  • SBA 7(a) rate and equity injection requirement at the time of application; whether the lender will blend the term across equipment and leasehold improvements.
  • Simulator quotes, installed, with the enclosure and screen included; software subscription costs per bay per year.
  • Whether a specific target parcel is in an Opportunity Zone or a qualified low-income census tract under the current designations.
  • Each school division's procurement threshold for a contract that can be signed without a board vote.
  • Insurance quotes that include liquor liability and youth programming; some carriers will not write both on one policy.
