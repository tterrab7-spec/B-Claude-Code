Series production plan · Harbor Line media

What He Told Everyone

A survivor-led short video series about coercive control, the story the abuser tells, and the pull that drags people back. Built for Erin to lead, on her terms. Three to twelve minutes an episode, made to be shared by the people who need it and the people who don't yet know they need it.

Working title (alternates: "No Bruises" · "The Calm One") Prepared Sept 2026 Host and creative lead: Erin
First principle

This is Erin's story and Erin's show

Everything below is a starting structure, not a script for her life. She decides what gets told, how much, when, and whether her face and name are on it. Nothing gets recorded, posted, or pitched without her yes, and her yes can be withdrawn at any point, including after something is live. Put that in writing between the two of you before the first shoot. Survivors who go public and later feel they lost control of their story get hurt twice.

The most useful thing you bring is what you're good at: structure, partners, production, money, and staying out of the frame.

Why this series

The story he told is the thing nobody has answered

Shelters teach safety planning. Therapists treat trauma. Nobody is systematically talking to the audience that decides whether a survivor gets believed: her friends, her family, the men in his circle, the officer at the door, the pastor, the judge. This series goes after that audience, in a format short enough to actually get watched and forwarded.

For the woman still inside it

Recognition. "That's my house." The single most common thing survivors say when they finally hear coercive control described is that they didn't know it had a name.

For the men around her

Most of his friends aren't villains. They heard one side, for years, from a guy who seemed fine. Give them a way to see it without being accused.

For the professionals

Police, GALs, clergy, HR. Three minutes on DARVO or on why the calm party isn't automatically the safe one changes how a call gets handled.

Format

Three tracks, one voice

Track A · 3–5 min · Erin to camera

The talk

One idea per episode, delivered straight, TED-style but without the stage. Opens with a specific moment, names the pattern, ends with one thing to do. This is the shareable unit.

Track B · 8–12 min · Erin + counselor

The conversation

Erin and a licensed trauma therapist or DV advocate work through one theme. She brings the lived experience, they bring the clinical frame. This is where the trauma-bond and "addiction" material lives, handled by someone qualified.

Track C · 3–5 min · for men

What you didn't see

Addressed to the friend, the brother, the coworker. Sometimes Erin, sometimes a male voice (you, a counselor, a survivor's brother, a former officer). No lecturing. "Here's what was happening while he was telling you she was crazy."

Sample

Episode 1 skeleton: "No Bruises"

A shape, not her words. Roughly 3 minutes 30 at a natural pace. Erin rewrites every line in her own voice or throws it out.

Cold open · 0:00–0:25 · one specific, ordinary moment

"Nobody ever hit me. I want to start there, because for a long time that's the sentence I used to tell myself it wasn't that bad."

Name it · 0:25–1:15

Coercive control, defined in one breath. Then three concrete things, delivered fast: who she was allowed to see, what happened to the money, how every argument ended with her apologizing for something she didn't do.

The turn · 1:15–2:15 · how she found out it had a name

The moment someone described it back to her. What it felt like to hear it. Why the name mattered more than sympathy.

To the viewer · 2:15–3:00

"If you've been keeping a list in your head of things that don't count because they didn't leave a mark, they count."

One action · 3:00–3:30

One number, one sentence. "You don't have to leave tonight. You just have to tell one person who isn't him." Number on screen. End.

Before she records anything

The safety and legal checklist

Going public is a risk decision, not a marketing decision. Work through this with Erin and with a lawyer before the first episode. Some of it is uncomfortable. All of it protects her.

Legal

  • Never name him, show his face, or include details that identify him to strangers. Truth is a defense to defamation, but a lawsuit still costs years and money, and it's a favorite tool of exactly this kind of man.
  • If any custody, support, or court matter involving him is open or could reopen, get a family lawyer's read before publishing. Public statements get used in court.
  • If her sons' father is in any way connected to this, their privacy and their relationship with him come first. Nothing about the boys without their say, at their age.
  • Write a one-page agreement between Erin and the production entity: she owns her story and likeness, can pull any episode at any time, and gets paid if this ever earns money.

Safety and wellbeing

  • Threat check first. Does he know where she lives? Has he ever stalked, threatened, or owned a gun? Run the Danger Assessment with an advocate before deciding how visible to be.
  • Decide the visibility level: full name and face, first name only, or voice and silhouette. All three work for this format.
  • Erin has her own therapist through the whole production, not just a guest counselor. Telling it on camera reopens it.
  • Comments are moderated by someone who isn't Erin. Abusers and their defenders show up in comments. She should never have to read them.
  • Lock down accounts: new emails, two-factor, no location metadata in uploads, no filming at home in ways that show the house.

Trauma-informed production standard. One story per session. She can stop mid-take. Nobody asks "what's the worst thing that happened." The counselor guest is briefed that they are there to frame, not to treat on camera. The crew is small and she picks them.

Production

Make it clean, quiet, and cheap to repeat

Look and sound

  • One fixed camera, one lav mic, soft natural light, a neutral room. No slick studio. Calm should be the whole aesthetic, because the subject is people who never got to be calm.
  • Vertical cutdowns (45–60 seconds) of every episode for short-form platforms, always ending on the number.
  • Captions on everything. Many people will watch with the sound off because he's in the next room.
  • Original score from The Healing Library catalog, cleared for use. Instrumental only under speech.

Who does what

  • Erin: host, final say on every cut.
  • You: executive producer, partners, funding, staying off camera unless Track C calls for it.
  • Amplifi Digital: editing, cutdowns, captions, distribution calendar.
  • Clinical partner: a licensed trauma therapist or advocate from Transitions, YWCA, or The Center as recurring Track B guest and script reviewer.
  • Legal: one review of each episode before release, ten minutes each once the rules are set.

Distribution

  • Own the home: a simple site with a quick-exit button, every episode, and the resource map. Harbor Line's site, or its own.
  • YouTube for the full episodes, Instagram and TikTok for the cutdowns, a podcast feed for Track B audio.
  • The distribution that matters most is offline. QR cards in DV program lobbies, courthouse self-help centers, OB-GYN and pediatric waiting rooms, salons, churches, base family centers. Ask each partner to play Episode 1 at a staff meeting.
  • Pitch Track C to men's groups, fraternities, athletic departments, and the region's big employers as a 5-minute training piece.
Next 60 days

How to start

  1. Week 1: Erin reads this and marks it up. She decides visibility level, which episodes she wants to tell, and which she doesn't. That list is the season.
  2. Week 2: Lawyer consult on the legal checklist. Danger Assessment with an advocate. Therapist lined up for the production run.
  3. Weeks 3–4: Recruit the clinical partner. Transitions or YWCA South Hampton Roads will know who's good on camera. Offer to credit the organization in every episode.
  4. Weeks 5–6: Record Episodes 1, 2, and 3 in one weekend. Edit. Legal review.
  5. Weeks 7–8: Soft launch to the partner organizations first, not the public. Ask what they'd change. Then release all three together with the resource map.

Don't measure this by views. Measure it by partners who show it to staff, cards picked up in lobbies, and messages that say "I didn't know it had a name." The first survivor who calls a hotline because she saw Erin say it out loud is the whole point.
